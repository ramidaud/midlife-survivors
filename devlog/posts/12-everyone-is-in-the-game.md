This one's going to be a little different. Most of what changed since the last memo didn't start with me. It started with other people taking the time to play the game and tell me what they honestly thought. So this memo is mostly about them.

## Paul's review

My coworker Paul sat down with the game and sent me notes, a screen recording, and some reading: [*Shovel Knight*](https://bossfightbooks.com/products/shovel-knight-by-david-l-craddock) by David L. Craddock, from Boss Fight Books, about how a small team made a modern classic. It felt really good to get real, honest, knowledgeable advice, and research materials to help me think more deeply about what I've gotten myself into.

What came out of it:

- **A steady walk.** The hero's legs used to speed up and slow down with the frame rate. Now they keep their own clock, and there are new walk cycles to go with it.
- **The Sprite Board.** Paul's idea: one page that shows every sprite in the game side by side, on any background color, with buttons to flip every card to the same pose. It's how I check new art now.
- **Cleaner coworkers.** The office crowd got a pixel cleanup.

One more on the reading list: [*Teaching the Game*](https://doi.org/10.17613/2j5eh-p5n15), edited by Rick Ferdig, Emily Baumgartner and Enrico Gandolfi. It collects real game design courses: how they run playtests, how students keep devlogs, and how AI fits into the work. It's free to read.

Emily is currently my creative coding professor in [EMAT](https://www.kent.edu/emat), and I took an AI workshop with Rick this summer as part of the [College of Aeronautics and Engineering](https://www.kent.edu/cae). Both professors are incredible, and if that's an indication of what the next few years have to offer, I'm excited.

And one I need to pick up: [*Game Programming for Artists*](https://bookshop.org/books/game-programming-for-artists/9781138106130) by Jarryd Huntley and Hanna Brady. Hanna is my good friend Damian's wife, and an accomplished veteran of the video game industry. A book that teaches game programming to people who think like artists sounds like it was written for me.

## The music

The game has a soundtrack now: a title theme, three moods that build as you get older, a boss track, and a retirement party at the end. It's AI-generated. I wrote the prompts, picked the takes I liked, and Claude trimmed and leveled them for the game. The sound effects were made the same way, replacing the old code-made beeps. If you miss those, there's a "Classic Beeps" switch in the pause menu.

The music came from Google's Flow Music. Here's the prompt for the title theme:

> 16-bit SNES-style chiptune title screen theme, catchy square-wave lead melody, soft bass, light drums, warm, a little wistful and optimistic, like coffee on a Monday morning, major key, 100 bpm, steady tempo, loops cleanly, no fade-out

The sound effects came from ElevenLabs. Here's the one for the boss defeat:

> Cartoon boss defeat, a comedic crash, a descending slide whistle, then a loud rubber stamp thump, retro game style

It still needs work, honestly. I want my son to make it, but he's still learning. And I have so many thoughts on the music that it's kind of hard to keep it holistic and chaotic.

## Kevin

Kevin is a friend from way back in high school. He found the game on Discord and started sending ideas, and three of them are in the game now:

- **Hustle Bonus.** Running around the map and exploring before 55 earns you more.
- **Burning the Candle.** After 55 the script flips. Too much running around fills a wear meter, and when it's full, a Medical Bill comes for you. From all those energy drinks, right?
- **Fiscal-year seasons and the Corporate Buyout.** The leaderboard resets with each season, and once a season you can cash out your upgrades for half price and start fresh.

Tonight he also asked if I had a language filter ready for the inevitable high scorer who goes ten steps beyond "Seymore Butts." I did not. Now I do. Swears and slurs get bounced at the door ("HR flagged that name"), and the database turns them away too. Semore Butts lives on, though.

Best part: this has turned into a way for us to reconnect after a long time. That alone made the whole project worth it.

## A stranger on Facebook

The fact that a random person commented on my personal Facebook post: it's public, but no one is really knocking down my door. And for the message to be so nice, from someone who'd obviously played through the game and given it some thought. That felt like a real honor.

His point: in a game like this, your only real job is reading the swarm, and Bills and 3 AM Thoughts read as one blob once there are 200 on screen. His fix was to make them move differently. So now Bills march straight in, and 3 AM Thoughts drift side to side on their way. Same size, same speed, and you can pick them out at a glance.

## Who's actually playing

I looked at the traffic for the first time this week. Most people play on their phones. Somebody in Taiwan plays a lot (that's my friend Ben).

In a world of social media, it's really cool that people took time out to try something that came out of some random new obsession of mine. Well, maybe not so random. This has been cooking in my head for a long time, but I kept getting overwhelmed by the coding. My brain tried, but it just doesn't hold on to it. Practice makes perfect, though, and hopefully I'll start to pull some real lessons for myself out of this as well.

I'm not great at sitting still, and this gives me somewhere to put it.
