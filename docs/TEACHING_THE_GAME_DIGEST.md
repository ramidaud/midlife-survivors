## 1. A playtest protocol we can use (adapted from Ch. 25 and Ch. 10)

The Kent State prototyping course has one team member stay with the game to run the test while everyone else plays other teams' games. Each project ends with critique from outside guests.

**Our version, per playtest (15 minutes):**
1. **Rami watches, doesn't help.** Hand over the phone, say only "play like you normally would."
2. **Note what you see, not what you think:** where they hesitate, what they misread, when they laugh, how they die.
3. **Ask the same three questions every time:**
   - What were you trying to do just before you died?
   - What did you not understand?
   - What made you laugh, or want to play again?
4. **Log it** in the Discord #playtest-notes channel or the progress log, one line per finding.
5. **Fix one thing, then retest** with someone new.

Rule from Ch. 10: feedback should be specific and usable, not polite. "The chest was confusing" is weak. "I thought the chest was an enemy and ran" is something we can act on.

## 2. MDA map: Midlife Survivors in one table (from Ch. 1)

**MDA** = Mechanics (the rules), Dynamics (what happens when you play), Aesthetics (what the player feels).

| Mechanic | Dynamic | What the player feels |
|---|---|---|
| Weapons fire on their own; you only move | Constant dodging and herding | Flow, "I'm just trying to survive the week" |
| Bills split into Late Fees if ignored | Pressure to deal with small things early | Recognizable adult stress, then relief |
| Age clock, 45 s per year | The swarm grows as you age | Midlife creeping up |
| Level-up: 3 cards, evolutions | Planning a build, chasing pairs | Cleverness, power fantasy by your 60s |
| Bosses on birthdays | Big set-piece fights | Dread, then triumph (the stamp) |
| Interns bring gifts | Running toward a friend mid-swarm | Warmth: other people help |

Use it when a feature feels off: if a mechanic doesn't produce the feeling in its row, change the mechanic, not the feeling.

## 3. Devlogs as reflection (Ch. 28, GDIM 32)

- The course pairs every coding assignment with a short Devlog essay about how the student planned and solved it.
- The author found that well-worded reflection questions drew fewer AI-written answers, because AI can't report your own thought process.
- **For us:** this is the case for building posts from Rami's voice memos and interviews. Good interview questions ask about his process and feelings ("what surprised you?"), not facts I already know.

## 4. Level design for the new stages (Ch. 28, AGD 34004)

- Treat a level like architecture: sketch a **plan** (from above) and a **section** (a slice from the side) before building.
- **Block out small encounters first,** then chain them into larger sequences of spaces.
- Design for the player's experience of the space: what they see first, where they're pulled, where they feel safe.
- Texts the course uses: Francis D.K. Ching, *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order*, and Chris Totten, *An Architectural Approach to Level Design* (CRC Press).
- **For us:** before ChatGPT draws the Hospital or Home kit, Rami sketches a plan with 3-5 "rooms" and one memorable landmark per room (the nurses' station, the cul-de-sac). Photographer advantage: shoot reference plans of real spaces.

## 5. AI in the course (many chapters)

- Most chapters now describe how AI fits the course. A common thread: assessments center on things AI can't do, like live design decisions, playtest observations and reflection.
- One Vol II course has students write rule sets first, then test them in conversation with an LLM (Claude is named), framing AI as a partner to argue with, not an oracle.
- **For us:** Rami's "How I make this" statement fits this framing. The game ideas, playtests and stories are his; Claude builds and explains; every post says so. Useful for EMAT papers about this project, too.
